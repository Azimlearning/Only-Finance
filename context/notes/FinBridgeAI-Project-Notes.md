## 4. The two directions in detail

**Quoting convention:** MYR per 1 USD (a higher number means a weaker MYR).
**Shared illustrative inputs (not market rates):** spot S₀ = 4.50; Malaysia interest rate 3.0% p.a.; US interest rate 4.5% p.a. (1 year); expected inflation Malaysia 2.0%, US 3.0%.

### 4.1 Core formulas

| Relationship | Formula | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| IRP forward | F = S₀ × (1 + i_MY) / (1 + i_US) | Forward contract rate implied by the interest differential |
| PPP expected spot | S₁ = S₀ × (1 + π_MY) / (1 + π_US) | Expected future spot from inflation differential |
| International Fisher Effect expected spot | E(S₁) = S₀ × (1 + i_MY) / (1 + i_US) | Expected future spot from the interest differential |
| Fisher (real rate) | real rate = (1 + i) / (1 + π) − 1 (approximately i ≈ r + π) | Links nominal interest to inflation |

**Important point to explain in the report and video:** IRP and the International Fisher Effect use the **same formula**. IRP gives the **forward contract rate**; IFE gives the **expected future spot rate**. Explain the conceptual difference in words so it does not look like a duplicate calculation. PPP gives a different number because it uses inflation, not interest.

### 4.2 Direction 1: SME Import/Export Budget Coach

**Problem.** A small Malaysian importer pays a supplier in USD but budgets in MYR. The owner does not know why the rate moves or how much to set aside, so costs overrun.

**Target user.** Owners and finance staff at SMEs with no finance training.

**Flow.**
1. The user enters invoice amount and currency, payment date and current spot rate.
2. The user enters assumptions for Malaysian and US interest rates and inflation.
3. The app shows the MYR cost under several FX views.
4. The app shows a plain-language explanation of what drives each result.
5. The user changes an input and compares the two outcomes.

**Worked example: USD 100,000 payable in 1 year** (all numbers verified by script, see Section 11):

| View | Rate (MYR/USD) | MYR cost |
|---|---|---|
| Spot today (reference) | 4.5000 | 450,000 |
| IRP forward (lockable via a forward contract) | 4.4354 | 443,541 |
| PPP expected spot | 4.4563 | 445,631 |
| Stress: MYR weakens | 4.7000 | 470,000 |

The stress case costs 20,000 MYR more than today's spot. That gap is what the user learns to budget for.

**Sensitivity comparison (required by the brief).** Raise Malaysian inflation from 2% to 4%: PPP expected spot moves from 4.4563 to 4.5437, and the MYR cost of USD 100,000 moves from 445,631 to 454,369. Second sensitivity: raise the US interest rate from 4.5% to 5.5%: IRP forward moves from 4.4354 to 4.3934 (cost 439,336 instead of 443,541).

**Hedging (optional extension).** The app compares the forward-locked cost with the stress case and explains the trade-off.

**Strengths.** Concrete problem; easy-to-identify market; all three variables connect through MYR cash flow.
**Risks.** The forward rate is only "implied"; real banks quote their own forwards. The limitations section must say so.

### 4.3 Direction 2: International Finance Decision Assistant

**Problem.** A first-time international investor sees different interest rates in two countries and cannot tell whether the gap is a real opportunity or simply offset by the exchange rate.

**Target user.** Beginner investors or finance students deciding between holding MYR and USD.

**Flow.**
1. The user enters the spot rate, the two interest rates and a forward rate quoted by a bank.
2. The app computes the IRP-implied forward rate.
3. The app compares it with the quoted forward and says whether IRP holds.
4. If it does not, the app shows the covered arbitrage direction and profit.
5. The app discusses expected currency changes using inflation (PPP) and the interest differential (International Fisher Effect).

**Worked example.** The IRP forward is 4.4354. Suppose a bank quotes 4.4000.
- Strategy: borrow USD 1, convert to MYR at 4.50, invest at 3.0%, buy USD forward at 4.40.
- 1 × 4.50 × 1.03 = 4.635 MYR, which buys 4.635 / 4.40 = **1.0534 USD**.
- The USD loan costs 1.045 USD, so the risk-free gain is about **0.0084 USD per USD borrowed**.
- The app then explains the gap should disappear as traders exploit it, which is why IRP holds in practice.

**Sensitivity comparison.** Widen the interest gap by raising the US rate to 5.5%; the forward moves further from spot.

**Strengths.** Most direct application of taught theory, good for finance accuracy.
**Risks.** More academic; weaker market case. Real arbitrage faces transaction costs and capital controls, so these must go in the limitations.

### 4.4 Comparison

| | Budget Coach | Decision Assistant |
|---|---|---|
| Real-world problem (15) | Stronger | Weaker |
| Marketability (10) | Stronger | Weaker |
| Finance accuracy (35) | Strong | Strong, more theory-heavy |
| Beginner-friendliness | High (cash-flow framing) | Medium |

A good combination: Budget Coach as the main app, with the arbitrage check from the Decision Assistant as an optional "Is this forward rate fair?" screen.

## 5. SME current state in Malaysia (with sources)

**Scale (2025).**
- Nearly 1.3 million SMEs operated in 2025, about 96% of all businesses, an increase of almost 20% over the previous year ([SME Corp Malaysia](https://www.malaysiasme.com.my/?p=11568)).
- SME GDP grew 5.7% in 2025 to almost RM690 billion, outperforming national growth of 5.2%. Contribution to national GDP rose from 39.5% (2024) to 39.7% (2025) (same source).
- SME exports rose 10.5% to RM214.5 billion, versus 5.9% national export growth and 5.1% for non-SME firms (same source).

**Distribution (2024, DOSM data via OECD).** Total MSMEs 1,086,386: microenterprises 761,897 (70.1%), small 306,764 (28.2%), medium 17,725 (1.6%). Services accounted for 917,070 establishments (84.4%) ([OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/financing-smes-and-entrepreneurs-2026_bb7fc5c4-en/malaysia_ebf7a4e9-en.html)). The 2024 count (about 1.09 million) differs from the "nearly 1.3 million" cited for 2025; the sources do not explain the gap, so cite each with its year.

**Export share.** MSMEs' share of national exports rose from 12% (2023) to 14.3% (2024) ([Malaysia SME](https://www.malaysiasme.com.my/?p=6757)).

**Most are tiny (inference).** Since most SMEs are microenterprises, most likely have no treasury or finance team. The sources do not say this directly; mark it as an inference.

**Exposed but only partly hedged.** An ACCA-Kantox report (2013) found that SMEs with international activities are significantly exposed to FX risk, that only part of it is hedged and that even less is actively managed. Reasons for not hedging were high costs, complexity of building a successful strategy, and collateral requirements ([ACCA](https://www.accaglobal.com/gb/en/member/member/accounting-business/2016/02/insights/ringgit-smes.html)). **Caveat:** this is a 2013 report, not Malaysia-specific; use it as general evidence.

**Skills gap.** UOB states that analysing and anticipating currency movements takes time and specific expertise that many SME managers lack ([UOB Malaysia](https://www.uob.com.my/business/sme-hub/insights/risk-of-import-export-trades.page)).

**Resource limits.** A WorldFirst blog says many Malaysian SMEs lack deep cash reserves and access to advanced hedging products such as derivatives ([WorldFirst](https://www.worldfirst.com/my/blog/international-transactions/reduce-fx-risks/)). **Caveat:** commercial vendor blog; lower reliability.

**Policy support (see Section 7 for the full BNM picture).** BNM's 2019 liberalisation let eligible SMEs that are net importers receive foreign-currency payment from resident exporters for domestic trade, a "natural hedge", recognising SMEs' limited hedging capabilities ([The Edge, 28 March 2019](https://www.theedgemarkets.com/article/bank-negara-further-liberalises-forex-administration-framework)).

**Data gap.** No Malaysia-specific statistic on the percentage of SMEs that hedge was found. **Do not put a made-up number on the poster.** Use the qualitative evidence above and cite it.

**Existing alternatives (competitor analysis).** Banks offer FX advisory and trade products (for example UOB's trade specialists and FX specialists), and fintechs such as WorldFirst offer multi-currency accounts. These mainly help SMEs *execute* trades. **Inference (label as such on the poster):** there is a gap for a free, bank-neutral tool that *explains why* the rate moves and what it means for the MYR budget, linking interest rates, inflation and FX in plain language.

## 7. Relating the coach to Bank Negara Malaysia (BNM)

### 7.1 Correction made during the session

An earlier answer said hedging was allowed up to 12 months without approval. That came from a **2019** article. In the same year BNM went further: effective 30 August 2019, **residents can hedge foreign currency current account obligations up to their underlying tenure**, compared with up to 12 months previously ([The Sun](https://thesun.my/business/bnm-liberalises-fea-policies-BY1274074)). The SME natural-hedge facility is also from the 2019 liberalisation, not recent.

### 7.2 BNM rules and how the coach reflects them

| BNM rule or role | What it means for an SME | How the coach reflects it |
|---|---|---|
| **Onshore hedging.** BNM and the Financial Markets Committee liberalised the onshore ringgit hedging market, including FX hedging for current and financial account transactions based on commitment ([The Sun](https://thesun.my/archive/2082695-FTARCH412510)). | An SME can lock a rate through a licensed onshore bank for a real trade commitment. | The hedge-or-not screen is framed as "a forward from your bank for a genuine invoice", not speculation. The tenor input should match the invoice period. |
| **Export repatriation.** An exporter of goods can be paid in ringgit or foreign currency but must repatriate proceeds in full within 6 months of shipment; up to 24 months only for reasons beyond the exporter's control and other permitted reasons ([BNM](https://www.bnm.gov.my/fep/policies/rules-by-residency/resident/export-of-goods)). Banks check this using documents such as the export invoice, bill of lading and customs K2 form ([ABM MDD7, updated 1 Nov 2025](https://www.abm.org.my/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/2025-11-01-MDD7_version-2025_Final.pdf)). | An exporter has a deadline for bringing the money home. | Exporter mode shows a 6-month repatriation reminder and a "convert now vs later" comparison. |
| **Conversion is the exporter's choice.** From 15 April 2021 BNM removed the export conversion rule; resident exporters manage conversion according to their foreign currency cash-flow needs ([BNM](https://www.bnm.gov.my/-/liberalisation-of-foreign-exchange-policy)). BNM said in 2023 that conversion is a business decision for exporters ([FMT](https://www.freemalaysiatoday.com/category/highlight/2023/08/18/up-to-exporters-to-convert-foreign-proceeds-earned-says-bnm)). | When to convert USD to MYR is a judgment call, exactly where FX forecasting matters. | A "convert now vs hold USD" scenario for exporters driven by the PPP/IRP views. |
| **Netting.** Resident exporters can net off export proceeds against permitted foreign currency obligations without BNM approval (BNM 2021 notice, above). | A firm that both imports and exports carries only the net exposure. | **Net exposure** feature: USD receivables minus USD payables, with the hedge sized on the net. |
| **Natural hedge for net-importer SMEs.** Eligible SMEs may apply to their banks to receive foreign currency payments for domestic trade with resident exporters (The Edge, 2019, above). | Some SMEs can avoid converting at all. | Mention as an extension or in the limitations. |
| **Rules change over time.** In December 2016 BNM required exporters to convert 75% of export revenue into ringgit, allowing 25% to be retained in foreign currency ([The Vibes](https://www.thevibes.com/index.php/articles/business/96059/bnms-call-to-compel-exporters-to-convert-proceeds-into-ringgit-zafrul)); this requirement was later removed (2021, above). | Regulations are not permanent. | The app shows a "rules last checked" note with a link to BNM's FEP page. Do not hard-code legal claims. |

**Other BNM history (context).** April 2020: exporters were exempted from converting proceeds below RM200,000 per transaction ([Malay Mail](https://www.malaymail.com/news/money/2020/04/30/bank-negara-refines-foreign-exchange-policy-to-improve-business-efficiency/1861781)). BNM data from 2023 showed exporters tended to convert about 60–70% of foreign proceeds (FMT, above).

**BNM and SME funding.** Since the pandemic, BNM has disbursed over RM29 billion in SME funding to 86,000 businesses ([The Vibes](https://www.thevibes.com/articles/news/113844/budget-2026-export-support-and-entrepreneur-empowerment-ramps-up)).

**BNM as a source of model inputs (suggestion).** BNM's policy rate is a reference point for the Malaysian interest-rate input; DOSM publishes Malaysian inflation; US equivalents are the Federal Reserve and BLS. Verify current figures at source, or label your numbers as illustrative.

**Verification gap.** No current official BNM page covering the hedging-tenor rule was found, only 2019 news reports. **Before putting it on the poster, check BNM's current Foreign Exchange Policy notices at bnm.gov.my/fep.**
